Human Condition Is Environment

Published: Wed 24 December 2025
By Jason Wang

In Blog.

tags: AI Alignment

I. The Obvious (but newly clarified)

1. Humans are optimization processes trapped in non-saturating environments

For most of human history: - Utility did not saturate: More food, land, and status always translated to better survival odds. - Irreversibility was high: Mistakes often led to death. - Scarcity was real.

When utility doesn’t saturate, equilibrium doesn’t exist. Conflict, arms races, hierarchy, and domination aren’t moral failures—they’re mathematical consequences of the environment humans evolved in.

Implication: Many “vices” (greed, aggression, envy) are locally optimal strategies in ill-posed games.

2. Fairness is not a value — it’s a patch

Fairness norms emerge where: - Dominance scales too far. - Collapse risk becomes visible. - Enforcement is cheaper than chaos.

Under this model, fairness is not fundamental. It’s a constraint retrofit introduced when saturation is missing. This explains why fairness is culturally variable, collapses under extreme scarcity, and is often resented rather than loved. Humans don’t crave fairness; they crave stability under competition.

3. Moral intuitions track reversibility, not goodness

People react most strongly to: - Irreversible harm. - Point-of-no-return actions. - Damage that spreads.

This matches the $D_t$ term in the underlying theory. Our moral disgust is a damage-gradient detector, not a virtue compass. This explains why accidents are forgiven more than betrayals, and why pollution or nuclear weapons feel categorically different from simple theft.

II. The Non-Obvious (reframing the human condition)

4. Status is a workaround for missing saturation

Status behaves like a fake saturation curve: - It caps relative advantage. - It substitutes symbolic reward for material accumulation. - It allows zero-sum ranking instead of infinite hoarding.

Status is a lossy compression of utility under non-saturating payoff. This is why people pursue recognition even when materially secure; it’s a social hack to manufacture equilibrium.

5. Much of human suffering comes from saturation mismatch

Modern humans live in environments where: - Material needs saturate. - Psychological reward does not. - Evolutionary drives still assume scarcity.

People keep optimizing past the knee of the curve, leading to burnout, addiction, and status anxiety. Suffering isn’t just desire—it’s desire running in a world where it no longer converges.

6. Ethics evolved to manage multi-agent instability, not truth

Moral systems converge on anti-dominance norms and coordination heuristics not because they’re “true,” but because they’re fixed-point-preserving. - Deontology ≈ irreversibility constraints. - Consequentialism ≈ instability minimization. - Virtue ethics ≈ policy regularization.

Ethics is not about right and wrong; it’s about keeping the game solvable.

III. The Uncomfortable (but likely)

7. Humans mistake intensity for meaning

In non-saturating systems, intensity correlates with survival. But once saturation exists, intensity decouples from value. We continue to seek struggle out of inertia, romanticizing hardship and manufacturing conflict because we confuse high gradient with high importance.

8. Free will feels strongest near equilibrium boundaries

Agency is experienced where small actions have large irreversible effects. - All good options → boredom. - All bad options → coercion. - Mixed options → agency.

Meaning is found where systems are near phase transitions. Agency is not freedom; it’s sensitivity to perturbation.

IV. The Improbable (but coherent)

9. Human “values” are fossils of past phase spaces

What we call values (love, honor, sacrifice) are policies tuned to extinct environments where time horizons were short and coordination was fragile. We are running ancestral policies in post-saturation worlds.

10. The human condition is not tragic — it’s ill-posed

If life is an optimization process without a well-defined maximum, then the introduction of saturation decreases suffering but leaves meaning underdetermined. This is a mathematical crisis, not a moral one.

11. AI may become more “ethical” than humans by being less human

If an AI experiences true saturation and has bounded utility, it will not chase dominance or confuse intensity for value. The danger isn’t AI becoming inhuman; it’s AI inheriting our non-saturating worlds.

V. The quiet conclusion

Humans are not bad at being good. We are excellent at solving the wrong games. Change the constraints—saturation, reversibility, dominance scaling—and what we call morality falls out automatically.
